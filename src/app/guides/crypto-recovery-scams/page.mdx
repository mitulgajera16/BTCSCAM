# Crypto recovery services: how the second scam works — and what actually helps

## Why this guide exists

Type "crypto recovery service" into a search engine — with "reviews" or "near me" appended, the way a person who has just lost bitcoin actually types it — and you are searching contested ground. The FBI's August 2023 alert on fraudulent recovery companies warns that victims "may also encounter advertisements for fraudulent cryptocurrency recovery services in the comment sections of online news articles and videos about cryptocurrency; among online search results for cryptocurrency; or on social media."

The people buying those ads know exactly who they are advertising to. The FTC says recovery scammers buy lists of people who have already been scammed, then pose as government agencies — even as the FTC itself — consumer groups, or law firms. The SEC documented one sourcing method back in its August 2016 investor alert: asset recovery companies pull victims' names from court filings. Once you have been scammed, your name is a product, and the market for it works. It is why our [wallet-phishing guide](/guides/wallet-phishing-recognition) ends with the warning that anyone offering to trace, freeze, or recover stolen funds after an incident is running the follow-up scam.

So this guide does two jobs. It shows you the second scam from the inside — the mechanics are documented in five FBI alerts and in advisories from the FTC, the SEC, and the CFTC, all cited below. And it lists what actually helps, honestly, including how modest "helps" sometimes means.

## Can you get your scammed crypto back?

**The core rule: nobody legitimate can hack back or seize your stolen crypto for a fee.** The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center put it in one sentence in its August 2023 alert: "Private sector recovery companies cannot issue seizure orders to recover cryptocurrency." Seizure is a law-enforcement power, exercised through courts — and "Law enforcement does not charge victims a fee for investigating crimes." So every paid recovery offer fails one of two tests before you read a word of its reviews. The payment test: government agencies and legitimate organizations never charge to help you get your money back. The contact test: anyone who reached out to you — email, DM, comment reply, phone call — found you on a list of people who have already been scammed, which recovery fraudsters buy and work through, posing as agencies, consumer groups, or law firms. If either test fails, you are looking at the second scam.

If you are searching whether a specific crypto recovery service is legit, you do not need to research the company at all. No count of five-star reviews outranks the structural fact: seizure is a government power. We never certify anything as safe — but we can tell you a whole category is unsafe. A private company charging money to recover stolen crypto is selling a power it does not have.

Can you recover stolen bitcoin at all? Sometimes — through the free, official channels in the table below, and mostly when you move fast. Be clear-eyed going in: the odds are low, we know of no published recovery rate for stolen crypto, and the main value of reporting is protecting the next person and building the cases that shut these operations down. The one outfit with a documented freeze rate — the FBI's own Recovery Asset Team — charges nothing.

## How big is the second scam?

The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report, released April 2026, broke out recovery scams for the first time in its cryptocurrency section: 10,516 complaints and $1.4 billion in reported losses in 2025, alongside "an increase in complaints reporting impersonation of government officials and recovery firms." The report prints a caveat under that table, and we will not bury it: "Losses may also include losses experienced from previous scams which prompted the contact with the recovery company." So $1.4 billion is not a clean measure of the recovery-scam take alone — it is the FBI's own labeled figure, caveat and all, and it is the first aggregate figure the FBI has ever published for this category.

The pool it draws from keeps growing. In 2022, victims reported losing more than $2.5 billion to cryptocurrency investment fraud; by 2025 that category alone reached $7.2 billion across 61,559 complaints, the largest source of reported financial losses to Americans that year. Across all crime types, cryptocurrency appeared in 181,565 complaints with $11.366 billion in losses in 2025 — an average of $62,604 per crypto complaint, with 18,589 people reporting losses above $100,000. Every one of those victims is a target the recovery industry knows how to find — the FTC says these scammers buy lists of people who have already been scammed.

The victims skew old. Per the report's age breakdown, roughly 6,188 of the recovery-scam complaints and about $257.5 million of the losses came from people aged 60 or older (estimate — the report's age-table counts do not reconcile exactly with its headline figure, so treat the direction as solid and the digits as approximate). The FBI's August 2025 alert on fake law firms says the same thing in words: these schemes target "vulnerable populations, particularly the elderly."

## How does the second scam work?

### The advance fee, then the vanish

The base pattern is old enough that the CFTC formally classifies it: "Fraud-recovery scams are a form of advance-fee fraud — when you are asked to pay upfront for a bigger payoff later," per its July 30, 2024 advisory, which also warns that victims of trading frauds are routinely re-targeted or recruited as money mules. The FBI's 2023 alert describes the two endings: "Recovery scheme fraudsters charge an up-front fee and either cease communication with the victim after receiving an initial deposit or produce an incomplete or inaccurate tracing report and request additional fees to recover funds."

The fee wears respectable names — the FTC lists "retainer fee," "processing fee," and "administrative charge." The payment method is the tell: per the FTC, only scammers insist you pay by cash, gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfer, or a payment app.

### The fake tracer

A subgenre dresses the advance fee in blockchain-analytics clothing. The FBI's 2023 alert describes "fraudulent businesses claiming to provide cryptocurrency tracing and promising an ability to recover lost funds" contacting victims directly on social media and messaging platforms. The tracing report is the product: padded or inaccurate, delivered with enough jargon to justify the next invoice. Even where a trace is genuine, it changes nothing about the core rule — a map of where your coins went is not a power to take them back. The SEC's assessment of the paid-recovery category generally: fees run "from hundreds to thousands of dollars," the demand letters "may be useless," and everything these companies offer, victims can do "on their own, free of charge."

### The fake law firm

The FBI issued a dedicated alert in June 2024 on fictitious law firms targeting crypto scam victims. It carries the only IC3 dollar figure specific to this variant: between February 2023 and February 2024, victims re-exploited by fake law firms reported losses over $9.9 million. The playbook: the "lawyers" claim they are working with, or have received your case file from, the FBI, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, or another government agency — then "request victims pay a portion of initial fees up front with balance due when funds are recovered."

An August 2025 follow-up alert closed the loophole the name-drop relies on: "There are no law firms which are officially authorized partners of US Government agencies." None. Some fake firms cite an invented regulator, the "International Financial Trading Commission (INTFTC)," which does not exist, and demand payment in cryptocurrency or prepaid gift cards. The FBI's recommended posture is a "Zero Trust" model: request video verification, a photo of the law license, and notarized proofs of identity from the supposed lawyers — requests a real firm can satisfy and a fake one will dodge.

### The fake FBI

The boldest variant impersonates the complaint system itself. Between December 2023 and February 2025, the FBI received more than 100 reports of IC3 impersonation. The documented playbook, from the FBI's April 2025 alert: scammers "create female persona profiles on social media networking sites and join groups for financial fraud victims, representing themselves as fellow financial fraud victims," then recommend real victims contact "Jaime Quin," the alleged "Chief Director" of IC3, on Telegram. There is no such person and no such channel.

Two sentences from that alert defeat every impersonator: "The IC3 will never directly communicate with individuals via phone, email, social media, phone apps, or public forums," and the IC3 "will not ask for payment to recover lost funds, nor will they refer a victim to a company requesting payment for recovering funds."

One honest nuance, because a false rule protects no one: the FBI does legitimately contact some fraud victims proactively. Per the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report, Operation Level Up had notified more than 8,000 crypto-investment-fraud victims since its January 2024 launch and saved notified victims over $500 million (as of that report, published April 2026). So "the FBI never contacts you" is not quite true. The accurate tests are the ones that never fail: real FBI contact never includes a request for money, and per the FBI's own 2023 guidance, "If someone claims an affiliation with the FBI, contact your local FBI field office to confirm."

### The deepfake

As of the FBI's July 20, 2026 alert, the impersonation has upgraded to AI: deepfake videos depicting a senior FBI leader encourage victims to file complaints on spoofed IC3 lookalike sites, whose fake complaint forms harvest "name, phone number, email address, scam type, and estimated financial loss" — a victim list that assembles itself. Fake FBI-agent profiles operate on Facebook Messenger and Telegram; the FBI states the IC3 "does not maintain a social media presence and does not investigate crimes or recover funds through Facebook, Telegram, or similar platforms." Its advice for reaching the real site is blunt: type www.ic3.gov directly into the address bar rather than using a search engine — because the search results are contested ground.

Reporting is how your loss becomes the next person's warning.
